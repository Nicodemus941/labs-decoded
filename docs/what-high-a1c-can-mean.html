A1C

What high A1C can mean

An A1C test shows your average blood sugar level over the past two to three months. A high result usually means blood sugar has been running higher than expected over time, not just on one single day.[1][2][3]

Short answer: A high A1C can point to prediabetes or diabetes, but one number is not the whole story. Providers may repeat the test or pair it with another glucose test, and they also consider whether conditions like anemia, pregnancy, kidney disease, liver disease, or certain hemoglobin variants could affect the result.[1][2][3][4][5]
Updated July 19, 2026

What A1C measures

MedlinePlus explains that glucose sticks to hemoglobin inside red blood cells. The A1C result shows what percentage of your hemoglobin is coated with glucose, which is why it reflects your average blood sugar over the last few months instead of a single moment.[1]

CDC and NIDDK describe A1C as a longer-view blood sugar test. That makes it useful when clinicians want to know how blood sugar has been trending over time, not just what it looked like on the morning of the blood draw.[2][3]

What counts as high

The most commonly used cutoffs are consistent across MedlinePlus, CDC, NIDDK, Mayo Clinic, and Cleveland Clinic.[1][2][3][4][5]

  • Below 5.7% is the usual normal range.[1][2][3][4][5]
  • 5.7% to 6.4% falls in the prediabetes range.[1][2][3][4][5]
  • 6.5% or higher is in the diabetes range.[1][2][3][4][5]

Even so, MedlinePlus notes that reference ranges are still a guide and should be interpreted in context. What matters most is how the result fits with the rest of the picture and whether follow-up testing confirms it.[1]

Why one high result may not be the final answer

NIDDK says that if you do not have symptoms and an A1C suggests diabetes or prediabetes, the result should be confirmed on a different day with another A1C or another diabetes test.[3] Mayo Clinic makes the same point, noting that a diabetes diagnosis is often confirmed with two blood tests.[4]

MedlinePlus also notes that providers often use more than one test when they are diagnosing diabetes. A single elevated A1C is useful information, but it may still need confirmation before it becomes a diagnosis.[1][3]

What can make A1C less accurate

A1C is helpful, but it is not perfect. MedlinePlus, CDC, NIDDK, Mayo Clinic, and Cleveland Clinic all describe situations where the number can read falsely high or otherwise be less reliable.[1][2][3][4][5]

  • Anemia or other conditions that affect red blood cells.[1][2][3][4][5]
  • Kidney failure or liver disease.[1][2][3]
  • Recent blood loss, transfusion, or dialysis.[2][3][4]
  • Pregnancy, especially when other diabetes testing is being considered.[1][2][3][4]
  • Hemoglobin variants, including some forms linked to sickle cell disease or thalassemia.[2][3][4][5]

That is one reason clinicians sometimes compare A1C with fasting glucose, oral glucose tolerance testing, or day-to-day blood sugar readings instead of relying on A1C alone.[1][3][5]

Why providers may order other tests too

An A1C test does not require fasting, which makes it convenient.[2][3][4] But NIDDK and MedlinePlus both note that clinicians also use fasting blood glucose tests and oral glucose tolerance tests when they need more detail or confirmation.[1][3]

  • A repeat A1C on a different day.[1][3][4]
  • A fasting blood glucose test.[1][3]
  • An oral glucose tolerance test, also called an OGTT.[1][3]
  • Review of symptoms, pregnancy status, medications, and blood conditions that could affect accuracy.[1][2][3][4][5]

Questions you may want to ask at follow-up

  • Was this result in the prediabetes range or the diabetes range?
  • Do I need repeat A1C testing or another glucose test to confirm it?
  • Could anemia, pregnancy, kidney disease, liver disease, or a blood disorder affect this result?
  • How does this A1C fit with any home blood sugar readings, symptoms, or other lab work?

FAQ

Does a high A1C always mean diabetes?

No. A high A1C can fall in the prediabetes range, and even a result in the diabetes range may need confirmation if symptoms are not obvious. NIDDK and Mayo Clinic both describe repeat testing as a normal part of diagnosis.[3][4]

Do you need to fast before an A1C test?

No. CDC, NIDDK, and Mayo Clinic all say you do not need to fast before an A1C test, although another test ordered at the same visit might have different instructions.[2][3][4]

Can A1C and fasting glucose disagree?

Yes. NIDDK says that sometimes an A1C can suggest diabetes when another blood glucose test does not, or the reverse. That is one reason clinicians repeat testing before making a diagnosis.[3]

Bottom line

A high A1C often means blood sugar has been elevated over the past few months, but the number still needs context. The most useful interpretation usually comes from looking at the cutoff range, symptoms, repeat testing, and any conditions that could make the result less accurate.[1][2][3][4][5]
