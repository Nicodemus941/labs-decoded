ALP

What high ALP can mean

ALP stands for alkaline phosphatase. It is an enzyme found throughout the body, with especially important sources in the liver, bile ducts, and bones. A high ALP result can be a clue that something is affecting one of those systems, but the result usually needs more context before it means much on its own.[1][2][3]

Short answer: A high ALP result can happen with liver or bile duct problems, but it can also come from bone-related causes. That is why clinicians often compare ALP with other tests before deciding what the number most likely means.[1][3][4][5][6]
Updated August 1, 2026

What ALP measures

MedlinePlus says an alkaline phosphatase test measures the amount of ALP in your blood. Higher amounts of ALP are found in the liver, bile ducts, and bones, which is why a high result can point in more than one direction.[1]

Cleveland Clinic makes the same point in plain terms: the two main sources of ALP in blood are the liver and bones, and an ALP test by itself cannot make a diagnosis.[3]

Why a high ALP result can be hard to interpret

A high ALP number does not automatically tell you where the enzyme is coming from. MedlinePlus says abnormal ALP levels can be a sign of liver disease, bone disorders, and some other conditions, but other tests are usually needed to identify the source.[1]

MedlinePlus also notes in its liver function testing guide that high ALP can reflect bone disease as well as liver disease. Providers usually interpret the result alongside symptoms, medical history, and other blood tests rather than treating one number as the whole answer.[4]

Common reasons ALP runs high

  • Liver or bile duct problems.[1][2][4][5]
  • Bone disorders or increased bone activity.[1][2][3][4][5]
  • Conditions that affect bone growth or bone repair.[1][3]
  • Some other gastrointestinal or kidney-related conditions.[1]

Mayo Clinic says higher-than-usual ALP levels may mean liver damage or disease, such as a blocked bile duct, or certain bone diseases. That is one reason ALP is often discussed with the rest of a liver panel instead of alone.[5]

How providers often narrow it down

One common next step is to compare ALP with other liver-related tests. A MedlinePlus GGT guide explains that a GGT test is often done with or after an ALP test because both ALP and GGT may be elevated in diseases affecting the liver and bile ducts, while only ALP may be elevated in bone disease.[6]

In other words, if ALP is high and GGT is also high, the source may be more likely to involve the liver or bile ducts. If ALP is high but GGT is normal, a bone-related explanation may get more attention.[6]

What else may be reviewed at follow-up

Mayo Clinic and MedlinePlus both describe ALP as part of a broader testing picture. Providers may look at bilirubin, AST, ALT, symptoms, medications, and whether the result stays high on repeat testing.[2][4][5]

  • Were AST, ALT, bilirubin, or GGT high too?
  • Could the result fit better with a bone-related issue or a liver-related one?
  • Does the result need repeat blood work or additional testing?
  • Could medicines or supplements be affecting the pattern?

FAQ

Does a high ALP result always mean a liver problem?

No. ALP can come from the liver, bile ducts, bones, and some other tissues, so a high result is not liver-specific by itself.[1][3][4]

Can bone problems raise ALP too?

Yes. MedlinePlus, Mayo Clinic, and Cleveland Clinic all describe bone disorders as a common reason ALP can run high.[1][3][5]

Why might a provider order GGT after a high ALP result?

MedlinePlus says a GGT test can help show whether the pattern points more toward the liver or bile ducts, because bone-related ALP elevations may not raise GGT.[6]
