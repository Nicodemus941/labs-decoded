ALT

What high ALT can mean

ALT stands for alanine aminotransferase, an enzyme found mainly in the liver. When liver cells are irritated or damaged, ALT can leak into the bloodstream, so a high result can be a clue that the liver needs a closer look.[1][2][3]

Short answer: A high ALT result can happen with liver inflammation or injury, but it is not a diagnosis by itself. Providers usually interpret ALT together with AST, the rest of the liver panel, symptoms, medicines, alcohol exposure, and whether things like exercise or supplements could have affected the result.[1][2][3][4][5]
Updated July 30, 2026

What ALT measures

MedlinePlus says an ALT blood test measures the amount of ALT in your blood. Because ALT is found mainly in the liver, the test is commonly used to help diagnose liver damage or disease.[1]

Cleveland Clinic makes the same point in practical terms: ALT goes up when liver cells are damaged, so clinicians use it to help assess liver health.[3]

Why a high ALT result does not give the whole answer

MedlinePlus and Mayo Clinic both stress that one ALT number does not tell the whole story. Providers usually compare ALT with other liver tests and may look at changes over time rather than treating one isolated result as the final answer.[1][2][4]

MedlinePlus also notes that the amount of ALT in your blood is not the same as the amount of liver damage. A very important part of interpretation is the pattern of the other tests that were ordered with it.[1][4]

Common reasons ALT runs high

  • Fatty liver disease, including MASLD or MASH.[2][5]
  • Hepatitis or other infections affecting the liver.[1][2][5]
  • Alcohol-related liver injury.[2][3][5]
  • Medicines, supplements, toxins, or nonprescription pain relievers such as acetaminophen.[1][2][5]
  • Cirrhosis, liver cancer, or reduced blood flow to the liver.[1][2][3]

A higher ALT is not always from the liver alone. Mayo Clinic says some increases can happen with severe muscle injury, heart failure, or heart attack, and Cleveland Clinic notes that ALT can also shift with intense exercise.[2][3]

What else providers look at with ALT

ALT is often ordered as part of liver function tests or a comprehensive metabolic panel. Those panels can include AST, ALP, GGT, bilirubin, albumin, and total protein.[1][4]

MedlinePlus says ALT is usually measured along with AST as part of a liver function panel, and it adds that ALT checks more specifically for liver damage because ALT is found mainly in the liver. Looking at the panel together helps providers understand the broader pattern instead of over-reading one number alone.[1][4]

Symptoms and context still matter

MedlinePlus says ALT testing may be ordered when someone has symptoms that can go with liver problems, such as nausea, fatigue, weakness, jaundice, belly pain or swelling, dark urine, pale stool, or itching.[1]

But abnormal results can also be temporary or influenced by recent exercise, stress, diet, body size, menstrual cycle, medicines, or supplements. That is why a high ALT result should be read in context, not as a diagnosis from one line on a report.[1][2][3][4]

Reference ranges vary by lab

There is no one universal cutoff for a normal ALT level. MedlinePlus Medical Encyclopedia lists a common range of 4 to 36 U/L, Cleveland Clinic gives one common reference range of 7 to 56 U/L, and Mayo Clinic lists 7 to 55 U/L for males and 7 to 45 U/L for females.[2][3][6]

The big takeaway is that your own lab report range matters. A result that looks slightly high on one report may be interpreted differently on another, especially if the rest of the liver panel is reassuring.[2][6]

Questions worth asking at follow-up

  • How high is ALT compared with my lab's own reference range?
  • Were AST, bilirubin, ALP, albumin, or other liver tests abnormal too?
  • Could a medicine, supplement, alcohol use, or recent intense exercise be part of the picture?
  • Do I need repeat labs, imaging, or other follow-up tests?

FAQ

Does a high ALT result always mean liver disease?

No. High ALT can be a sign of liver inflammation or injury, but MedlinePlus, Mayo Clinic, and Cleveland Clinic all note that many other factors can affect the result, including medicines, supplements, exercise, stress, and the rest of your health context.[1][2][3][4]

Is ALT the same as AST?

No. Both are enzymes often measured together, but MedlinePlus says ALT is found mainly in the liver and is used more specifically to check for liver damage.[1]

Can exercise or medicines raise ALT for a short time?

Yes. Mayo Clinic says slight changes in ALT are common and recent vigorous exercise, a fatty meal, or some medicines may contribute. MedlinePlus and Cleveland Clinic also note that medicines, supplements, and intense exercise can affect the number.[1][2][3]
