Bilirubin

What high bilirubin can mean

Bilirubin is a yellow pigment your body makes as it breaks down old red blood cells. Your liver normally processes bilirubin into bile so it can leave the body through the digestive tract.[1][2][3]

Short answer: A high bilirubin result can happen when your body is making more bilirubin than usual, when your liver is struggling to process it, or when bile is not draining normally. That is why one high result is a clue that needs context, not a diagnosis by itself.[2][3][4][6]
Updated August 4, 2026

What bilirubin measures

MedlinePlus and Mayo Clinic both describe bilirubin as a normal breakdown product from old red blood cells. A bilirubin blood test checks how much of that pigment is in your blood and is often used to help assess liver health or to look into jaundice.[1][2]

Cleveland Clinic notes that bilirubin is commonly reported as total bilirubin, with direct and indirect pieces sometimes listed separately. Those extra details can help explain why the number is high.[3]

What counts as high

Cleveland Clinic says many labs consider total bilirubin around 0.2 to 1.3 mg/dL normal for older children and adults, but ranges can vary by lab. A flagged result matters most when it is read together with the lab's own reference range and the rest of the testing pattern.[3]

Common reasons bilirubin runs high

High bilirubin does not point to one single cause. Cleveland Clinic says it can rise if red blood cells are being broken down too quickly, if the liver is under stress or affected by liver disease, or if bile cannot flow out normally because of a bile duct or gallbladder problem.[3]

  • Extra red blood cell breakdown, such as hemolysis.[3][6]
  • Liver inflammation, injury, or chronic liver disease.[2][3][4]
  • Blocked bile flow from the liver or bile ducts.[2][3][4]
  • A mild inherited pattern such as Gilbert syndrome.[3][5][6]

Mayo Clinic also notes that higher bilirubin levels can happen when bile drainage is blocked, and MedlinePlus says bilirubin can build up in blood if the liver or bile ducts are not handling it normally.[1][2][4]

Why direct and indirect bilirubin matter

Cleveland Clinic's liver-test guide explains that not every bilirubin pattern points in the same direction. When most of the elevated bilirubin is unconjugated (indirect), the classic explanations are hemolysis or Gilbert syndrome rather than a primary liver disease pattern.[6]

That same Cleveland Clinic guide says conjugated (direct) bilirubin in blood points more toward liver disease, and bilirubin showing up in urine also leans toward a liver or bile duct problem because unconjugated bilirubin does not appear in urine.[4][6]

What symptoms or follow-up clues often matter

MedlinePlus says bilirubin can cause jaundice when it builds up enough to turn the skin or eyes yellow. Its urine bilirubin guide also lists dark urine, light-colored stool, itching, fatigue, nausea, and abdominal swelling or pain among the symptoms that may lead to follow-up testing.[1][4]

If bilirubin is found in urine, MedlinePlus says that can be an early sign of a liver condition. It also notes that providers often add a liver panel, because bilirubin is only one part of the broader picture.[4]

  • Was the bilirubin mostly direct or indirect?[3][6]
  • Were other liver tests high too?[2][4]
  • Was bilirubin found in urine?[4][6]
  • Is there a known Gilbert syndrome pattern or a possible blood-cell cause?[3][5][6]

FAQ

Does high bilirubin always mean liver disease?

No. High bilirubin can reflect liver or bile duct problems, but Cleveland Clinic says it can also happen when red blood cells are breaking down faster than usual or when someone has Gilbert syndrome.[3][5][6]

What is Gilbert syndrome?

Cleveland Clinic describes Gilbert syndrome as a mild inherited condition that can raise bilirubin because the liver does not process it as efficiently. Dehydration, fasting, illness, stress, menstruation, and overexertion can make the bilirubin rise more noticeably.[5]

What other tests may come up after a high bilirubin result?

Follow-up often includes a liver panel, urine bilirubin testing, and a look at whether the elevation was mostly direct or indirect, because that helps narrow the pattern.[3][4][6]
