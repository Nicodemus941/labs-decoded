Calcium

What high calcium can mean

A calcium blood test measures how much calcium is circulating in your blood. Calcium matters for nerve signaling, muscle function, heart function, hormone release, and blood vessel activity. Most of the calcium in your body is stored in bones and teeth, while a much smaller amount is in the blood.[1]

Short answer: A high calcium result, also called hypercalcemia, can happen with parathyroid disorders, certain cancers, dehydration, some medicines or supplements, and a smaller group of other medical conditions. The number alone does not show which pattern is responsible.[1][2][3]

Updated: August 10, 2026

What the test is really measuring

A routine total calcium test measures all the calcium in your blood, including calcium attached to proteins and calcium that is circulating freely. MedlinePlus explains that these two forms are normally both present and that a total calcium result is usually a good estimate of the blood calcium picture.[1]

That is also why one result may need more context than people expect. MedlinePlus notes that an ionized calcium test may be used when a total calcium result is abnormal or when protein binding may make the total number less straightforward.[1] Labcorp also emphasizes that total calcium should be interpreted with serum protein levels in mind because protein shifts can change the total number without changing the active, ionized fraction.[3]

Common patterns behind high calcium

  1. Primary hyperparathyroidism. MedlinePlus lists overactive parathyroid glands as a common cause of high calcium, and Cleveland Clinic says hypercalcemia is often caused by primary hyperparathyroidism.[1][2]
  2. Cancer-related hypercalcemia. Official sources list certain cancers, including cancers that spread to bone, among the better-known causes of hypercalcemia.[1][2][3]
  3. Dehydration, medicines, or supplements. Labcorp says dehydration is a very common reason for slight calcium elevations and also lists medicines such as lithium or thiazide-type diuretics, along with high vitamin D or vitamin A exposure, among possible contributors.[3]
  4. Other endocrine, kidney, or inflammatory patterns. MedlinePlus and Labcorp both note that calcium abnormalities can be tied to kidney disease, thyroid or parathyroid disorders, and other systemic conditions rather than to one single explanation.[1][3]

Symptoms can be absent or fairly broad

Many people with high calcium have no obvious symptoms at first. MedlinePlus says abnormal calcium levels may be found on routine panels, and it specifically notes that many people with high calcium levels do not have symptoms.[1]

When symptoms do happen, MedlinePlus lists digestive symptoms, kidney stones, and bone, joint, or muscle symptoms such as weakness, aches, and pains.[1] Cleveland Clinic also describes hypercalcemia symptoms as broad rather than unique to one disease, which is one reason follow-up testing usually matters.[2]

Why one high result usually needs context

A high calcium result is usually the start of interpretation, not the end of it. Providers often want to know whether the result was total or ionized calcium, whether albumin or other blood proteins were unusual, and whether there are clues pointing toward parathyroid, kidney, medication, or cancer-related patterns.[1][3]

In practical terms, follow-up questions often include:

  • Was this a total calcium result, and do protein levels make it harder to read?
  • Are calcium or vitamin D supplements, lithium, or thiazide-type diuretics part of the picture?
  • Are there symptoms such as constipation, nausea, thirst, frequent urination, weakness, or kidney stone history?
  • Do related labs suggest a parathyroid pattern, kidney issue, or another systemic cause?
  • Does the calcium stay high on repeat testing?

Labcorp specifically notes that repeated measurements and related follow-up testing can matter in the differential diagnosis of hypercalcemia, especially when trying to separate parathyroid patterns from other causes.[3]

One more important limitation

A blood calcium test does not tell you how much calcium is inside your bones. MedlinePlus says bone health is measured differently, such as with a bone density scan, so a high calcium result is not the same thing as knowing whether bones are strong or weak.[1]

FAQ

Can a high calcium result happen without symptoms?

Yes. Many people with high calcium do not notice symptoms at first. That is one reason the result is often found on a routine metabolic panel instead of during a dramatic medical event.[1]

Does high calcium always mean I got too much calcium from food?

No. Official sources list many possible causes, including hyperparathyroidism, certain cancers, dehydration, medicines, supplements, and other medical conditions. Food alone is not the default explanation.[1][2][3]

Why might albumin or ionized calcium matter?

Because routine total calcium includes calcium attached to proteins. If protein levels are unusual, the total number can be misleading, which is why clinicians sometimes look at ionized calcium or related protein values for context.[1][3]
