Creatinine

What high creatinine can mean

Creatinine is a waste product from normal muscle use. Your kidneys usually filter it out of your blood and remove it in urine.[1][3]

Short answer: High creatinine often points to a change in how well the kidneys are filtering, but it can also rise from dehydration, intense exercise, muscle injury, meat intake, or some medicines and supplements. That is why clinicians usually read creatinine together with other tests instead of using one number alone.[1][3][5]

What creatinine measures

MedlinePlus and Mayo Clinic both describe creatinine as a normal waste product tied to muscle activity. A creatinine blood or urine test helps show how well your kidneys are clearing that waste from the body.[1][3]

This makes creatinine useful, but not complete by itself. MedlinePlus notes that kidney problems can still be present even when creatinine is not yet far outside range, which is one reason follow-up testing may matter.[1]

What can push creatinine higher

A high creatinine result has more than one possible explanation. MedlinePlus lists kidney disease or injury among the common reasons, including reduced blood flow to the kidneys, urinary blockage, and kidney failure. Cleveland Clinic also notes that dehydration can temporarily raise creatinine, and MedlinePlus says intense exercise, muscle disorders, muscle injury, and a diet high in meat can affect the number too.[1][2][5]

  • Kidney disease or kidney injury.
  • Dehydration or reduced blood flow to the kidneys.
  • A blockage somewhere in the urinary tract.
  • Recent intense exercise or muscle breakdown.
  • Diet factors such as recent meat intake.
  • Some medicines or supplements that affect the test result.

MedlinePlus also points out that certain conditions such as heart failure or diabetes can affect the kidneys and show up through creatinine changes. The main idea is that high creatinine is a clue, not a final label.[1]

Why one high result is not a diagnosis

MedlinePlus says a single high creatinine result cannot diagnose a specific condition on its own. Retesting or pairing it with other kidney tests is often the next step when the result does not fit the full clinical picture.[1]

Cleveland Clinic makes a similar point: test results can be influenced by collection issues, high-intensity exercise, muscle mass, diet, pregnancy, and medications. That is why your provider usually looks at trend, context, and the rest of the labs instead of reacting to one isolated number.[5]

What other tests may be considered

Creatinine is commonly used to estimate eGFR, which helps show how well the kidneys are filtering. Mayo Clinic notes that eGFR can give a more accurate reading of kidney function because creatinine varies from one person to another.[3]

NIH's NIDDK says providers often pair creatinine-based testing with a urine albumin check, including the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, because albumin in the urine can be another sign of kidney damage.[4]

  • Repeat creatinine testing.
  • eGFR.
  • Urine albumin or urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio.
  • Other blood or urine tests based on symptoms and history.

How to read the reference range

MedlinePlus says your usual creatinine level depends partly on how much muscle you have, what you eat, your age, and how active you are. Cleveland Clinic adds that both high and low muscle mass can change how the number looks, which is another reason one person's result does not always translate cleanly to someone else.[1][5]

The range printed on your own lab report matters, and trend over time often matters even more than one isolated result.[1][3]

Questions you may want to ask at follow-up

  • Was creatinine the only kidney-related result outside range, or did eGFR or urine albumin change too?
  • Could dehydration, exercise, supplements, or recent diet have affected this test?
  • Do I need repeat blood work or a urine test for more context?
  • How does this result fit with my medications, symptoms, and past kidney-related labs?

FAQ

Does a high creatinine result always mean kidney disease?

No. MedlinePlus says high creatinine can reflect kidney disease or injury, but it can also rise with dehydration, intense exercise, muscle injury, or a diet high in meat.[1]

Why is eGFR reported with creatinine?

Because creatinine helps estimate GFR. Mayo Clinic says eGFR can offer a more accurate reading on kidney function since serum creatinine varies from person to person.[3]

What other tests may matter after a high creatinine result?

NIDDK says providers often check urine albumin alongside creatinine-based testing, and MedlinePlus says retesting or added kidney tests may be needed when a creatinine result is abnormal.[1][4]
