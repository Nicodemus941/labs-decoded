CRP

What high CRP can mean

CRP stands for C-reactive protein. It is made by the liver and rises when there is inflammation in the body.

Short answer: A high CRP result usually means your body is reacting to inflammation, but CRP alone cannot tell you exactly why. Providers often read it alongside your symptoms, history, and other tests.
Updated July 31, 2026

What CRP measures

MedlinePlus says a CRP test measures the level of C-reactive protein in your blood. Your liver makes CRP in response to inflammation, so the test is often used as a general signal that something inflammatory may be going on.

Cleveland Clinic describes CRP as a broad inflammation marker. That matters because it can be useful without being specific.

What a high result can and cannot tell you

A high CRP result suggests inflammation, but it does not identify the exact cause. MedlinePlus explains that CRP can show that inflammation is present somewhere in the body, while Mayo Clinic notes that clinicians often need other tests to work out the reason.

That is why one high CRP result is not a diagnosis by itself. It is a clue that needs context.

Common reasons CRP can go up

MedlinePlus, Mayo Clinic, and Cleveland Clinic all describe CRP as a marker that can rise with many different problems, from infections to chronic inflammatory diseases.

  • Infections, including some serious bacterial infections.
  • Autoimmune or inflammatory conditions such as lupus or rheumatoid arthritis.
  • Inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Injury or recovery after surgery or another invasive procedure.
  • Other ongoing inflammatory states that need more evaluation.

MedlinePlus also notes that smoking and exposure to some environmental toxins can raise CRP. Cleveland Clinic adds that providers sometimes use CRP to help monitor how inflammation changes over time.

CRP versus hs-CRP

Standard CRP and high-sensitivity CRP are related but not interchangeable. MedlinePlus says they are used for different situations. Mayo Clinic explains that hs-CRP can pick up smaller increases in CRP and is often used to help estimate heart disease risk.

Mayo Clinic also points out that even hs-CRP does not show the cause of inflammation. A higher value does not automatically mean there is a heart problem, because inflammation from other causes can affect the result too.

Why follow-up testing may happen

Because CRP is nonspecific, a clinician may pair it with other labs or repeat testing. MedlinePlus says providers interpret CRP together with symptoms, medical history, and other test results.

  • Other inflammation markers, such as ESR.
  • Tests aimed at infection when symptoms suggest one.
  • Condition-specific testing based on the rest of your history and exam.

How to read the report

Cleveland Clinic notes that lab reports usually show both your result and the reference range used by that lab. MedlinePlus says healthy people usually have very low CRP levels, but the result still needs clinical context to be useful.

The most helpful question is not just whether CRP is high, but what else was happening at the same time.

Questions you may want to ask at follow-up

  • Was CRP ordered to look for infection, to monitor inflammation, or for another reason?
  • Do my symptoms or other lab results point toward a likely cause?
  • Was this a standard CRP test or an hs-CRP test?
  • Do I need repeat testing or different tests to narrow this down?

FAQ

Does a high CRP result tell you exactly what condition you have?

No. MedlinePlus says CRP is a general test for inflammation, and Mayo Clinic says the cause often has to be clarified with other information and testing.

Can infection raise CRP?

Yes. MedlinePlus and Cleveland Clinic both list infections as a common reason CRP can rise, especially when a bacterial infection is significant.

Is CRP the same as hs-CRP?

No. MedlinePlus and Mayo Clinic explain that hs-CRP is a more sensitive version of the test that is often used to estimate heart disease risk rather than to answer every question about general inflammation.
