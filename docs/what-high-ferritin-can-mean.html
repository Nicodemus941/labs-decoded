Ferritin

What high ferritin can mean

Ferritin is a blood protein that stores iron. A ferritin test helps show how much iron your body has stored, but a high result does not automatically mean you have iron overload.

Short answer: High ferritin is often linked to inflammation, liver disease, or other medical conditions, and less often to iron overload itself. That is why clinicians usually interpret ferritin alongside other blood tests and the rest of your clinical picture.
Updated July 17, 2026

What ferritin measures

MedlinePlus describes ferritin as a protein that binds to iron and stores it in your body. A ferritin blood test measures the ferritin level in your blood and helps estimate how much iron your body has in reserve.

Ferritin is useful, but it is only one piece of the puzzle. MedlinePlus notes that providers may order ferritin as part of a larger group of iron tests, not as a stand-alone answer to every abnormal iron-related result.

What can push ferritin higher

A high ferritin result can happen for more than one reason. Mayo Clinic says it most often reflects inflammation. Mayo and Cleveland Clinic also list liver disease among common possibilities.

  • Inflammation from a wide range of conditions.
  • Liver disease or liver injury.
  • Autoimmune conditions or infections that raise inflammation.
  • Some chronic illnesses, including kidney disease and diabetes.
  • Iron overload conditions such as hemochromatosis.
  • Some cancers can also raise ferritin.

Cleveland Clinic also notes that alcohol-related liver injury and fatty liver disease can be part of the picture. The big takeaway is that ferritin can rise when the body is storing extra iron, but it can also rise when the body is under stress or inflammation.

Why one high result is not a diagnosis

A single ferritin number cannot tell the whole story. Cleveland Clinic says an abnormal ferritin level does not always mean something serious is wrong, and temporary conditions or even test variation can affect results.

Mayo Clinic makes the same point in a different way: if ferritin is high, a clinician may need other test results to figure out the next steps. That is especially important because inflammation and iron overload can both raise ferritin, but they are not the same thing.

What other tests may be considered

MedlinePlus explains that iron testing often includes more than one lab. In addition to ferritin, iron panels may include serum iron, transferrin, and total iron-binding capacity (TIBC).

When clinicians are specifically checking for iron overload, Mayo Clinic says transferrin saturation is a key screening test. If blood iron measures are higher than expected, additional testing may include repeat blood work, liver function tests, genetic testing, or imaging when appropriate.

  • Serum iron
  • Transferrin saturation
  • Total iron-binding capacity (TIBC)
  • Liver function tests in selected cases

How to read the reference range

Reference ranges are not completely universal. Cleveland Clinic says labs may use different normal ferritin ranges, and Mayo Clinic also notes that testing sites may use different cutoffs for what counts as high.

That means the range printed on your own lab report matters. A number that is flagged in one lab may not be flagged the same way somewhere else.

Questions you may want to ask at follow-up

  • Was ferritin the only abnormal iron-related test, or were other iron labs abnormal too?
  • Could inflammation, liver issues, or another condition be affecting this result?
  • Do I need repeat testing or a fuller iron panel?
  • How does this result fit with my symptoms, medications, supplements, and history?

FAQ

Does high ferritin always mean iron overload?

No. Mayo Clinic says high ferritin most often reflects inflammation, and Cleveland Clinic notes that infections, autoimmune conditions, liver disease, and chronic illness can also raise it.

Is ferritin the same as iron?

No. Ferritin is a storage protein for iron. MedlinePlus explains that ferritin helps show how much iron is stored in the body, while other iron tests measure different parts of iron transport and availability.

Why might my provider order more tests after a high ferritin result?

Because ferritin can rise for several different reasons. Mayo Clinic says clinicians may need the results of other tests to work out what the ferritin result means in context.
