GGT

What high GGT can mean

GGT stands for gamma-glutamyl transferase. It is an enzyme found in several parts of the body, but it is found mainly in the liver. A GGT blood test measures how much of that enzyme is in your blood.[1]

Short answer: A high GGT result can point toward liver or bile duct stress, but it does not diagnose one exact problem by itself. Providers usually read it together with ALP, AST, ALT, bilirubin, symptoms, medicines, and alcohol exposure before deciding what it means.[1][2][3][5]
Updated August 6, 2026

What GGT measures

MedlinePlus says GGT may leak into the bloodstream when the liver or bile ducts are damaged. That is why the test often comes up in liver panels or when another liver enzyme is abnormal.[1][5]

Cleveland Clinic also notes that GGT is one of the liver enzymes that can go up when liver tissue is injured or when bile flow is affected.[3][5]

What counts as high

There is no one universal cutoff. Cleveland Clinic says the normal range is often below 50 U/L, while Mayo Clinic and Labcorp both note that reference ranges vary by lab, age, and sex.[2][3][4]

That means a flagged result should always be read against the reference range printed on your own report, not against a number copied from somewhere else online.[2][3][4]

Common reasons GGT runs high

A high GGT result does not point to only one cause. MedlinePlus, Mayo Clinic, and Cleveland Clinic all list liver disease and bile duct problems as major reasons, but they also describe other conditions and exposures that can push the number up.[1][2][3]

  • Blocked or irritated bile ducts, sometimes called cholestatic patterns.[1][2][3]
  • Hepatitis, cirrhosis, or other liver inflammation or scarring.[1][2][3]
  • Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (fatty liver disease).[2][3]
  • Alcohol exposure or alcohol use disorder.[1][2][3]
  • Side effects from certain medicines, including some seizure medicines.[1][2][3][4]
  • Pancreatitis, diabetes, heart failure, or kidney failure in some cases.[1][2][3]

The key point is pattern recognition: the result may suggest that the liver or bile system is under stress, but it does not reveal the exact source by itself.[1][2][3]

Why GGT is often read with ALP

One of the most useful things about GGT is how it helps interpret a high ALP result. MedlinePlus says that if both ALP and GGT are elevated, a liver or bile duct source becomes more likely. If ALP is high but GGT is normal, a bone source becomes more likely.[1]

That is one reason providers often avoid reading a GGT result in isolation. Liver panels work better as a pattern than as a set of unrelated numbers.[1][5]

What else can affect the number

MedlinePlus says alcohol, smoking, medicines, supplements, and whether you have eaten recently can affect GGT results. Labcorp also notes fasting and medicine effects in its test guidance.[1][4]

  • Have AST, ALT, bilirubin, or ALP changed too?[1][5]
  • Could a medicine or supplement be affecting the liver test pattern?[1][2][3][4]
  • Was there recent alcohol exposure before the blood draw?[1][2][3]
  • Are symptoms such as jaundice, itching, dark urine, abdominal swelling, or pain part of the picture?[1][5]

Mayo Clinic says a provider may repeat the test, review medicines and alcohol use, or order more blood work or imaging if the result stays high.[2]

FAQ

Does high GGT always mean alcohol-related liver disease?

No. Alcohol can raise GGT, but MedlinePlus, Mayo Clinic, and Cleveland Clinic all list other possible causes too, including bile duct blockage, hepatitis, fatty liver disease, medicines, pancreatitis, diabetes, and heart failure.[1][2][3]

Why do providers compare GGT with ALP?

GGT can help show whether a high ALP result is more likely coming from the liver or bile ducts instead of bone. High ALP with high GGT leans more toward a liver or bile pattern, while high ALP with normal GGT makes a bone source more likely.[1]

Can one high GGT result diagnose the cause by itself?

No. A GGT result can suggest liver or bile system stress, but it cannot tell exactly why it is high. Providers usually interpret it with the rest of the liver panel, the clinical picture, and sometimes imaging.[1][2][3][5]
