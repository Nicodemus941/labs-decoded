What high LDH can mean

LDH, short for lactate dehydrogenase, is an enzyme found in many parts of the body, including muscles, liver, kidneys, and red blood cells.[1][2] Because damaged cells can release LDH into the blood, the test is often used as a broad clue that tissue damage may be happening somewhere.[1][2]

Short answer: A high LDH result means the level is above the lab's expected range. It can happen with many different conditions and injuries, so the number by itself does not diagnose one specific problem or show exactly where the source is.[1][2][3] Providers usually compare it with other tests and your clinical context before drawing conclusions.[1][2]

What LDH does in a blood test

LDH helps your cells make energy and is present in tissues throughout the body.[1][2] That is why a total LDH result is not tied to only one organ system.

MedlinePlus says an LDH test is mainly used to check for tissue damage, and Cleveland Clinic notes providers also use it to help diagnose or monitor several different conditions.[1][2]

What counts as high

Cleveland Clinic says labs may use different reference ranges for LDH, and the normal adult ranges are often around 135 to 225 U/L for males and 135 to 214 U/L for females.[2] Children can have higher normal LDH levels than adults.[2]

A report can be flagged as high even when the change is modest, so it helps to look at the exact range printed beside your own result.[2]

High LDH is a clue, not a diagnosis

A total LDH test can suggest that tissue damage is present, but it cannot show what is causing the problem or where in the body it is coming from.[1][2]

MedlinePlus explains that providers often order other tests alongside LDH, and an LDH isoenzymes test may sometimes help narrow down which tissues are involved.[1][3]

Many different conditions can raise LDH

Both MedlinePlus and Cleveland Clinic list a wide range of possible causes for high LDH rather than one single explanation.[1][2]

  • Anemia or other blood-related problems.[1][2]
  • Liver, kidney, or lung disease.[1][2]
  • Muscle injury, bone fracture, or other tissue injury.[1][2]
  • Infections such as meningitis, encephalitis, mono, or HIV.[1][2]
  • Certain cancers or monitoring how cancer treatment is working.[1][2]

That broad list is why LDH is better understood as a context marker than as a one-number answer.[1][2]

Exercise, medicines, and sample issues can affect the number

A high LDH result does not always mean a disease is present. MedlinePlus and Cleveland Clinic both note that strenuous exercise can raise LDH, and Cleveland Clinic also says some medicines may affect the result.[1][2]

Both sources also note that LDH can look falsely high if red blood cells break open during sample collection or handling.[1][2] That is another reason follow-up interpretation matters.

Questions worth asking at follow-up

If a high LDH result showed up on your report, these grounded follow-up questions can help:

  • Which other tests moved with it, such as CBC, liver tests, or kidney-related labs?
  • Was there any chance the sample was affected by red blood cell breakdown?
  • Would repeat testing or more specific follow-up labs help explain the result?

FAQ

Does high LDH always mean cancer?

No. High LDH can happen with many noncancer conditions, including anemia, liver disease, kidney disease, lung disease, muscle injury, and infections.[1][2] Providers use it as one clue, not as a diagnosis by itself.[1][2]

Can exercise raise LDH?

Yes. MedlinePlus and Cleveland Clinic both note that strenuous exercise can raise LDH, which is one reason providers look at the rest of the picture before deciding what the result means.[1][2]

Can an LDH test show which organ is affected?

Not by itself. A total LDH test can show tissue damage may be present somewhere, but it cannot show where the source is.[1][2] Providers may use other tests, and sometimes an LDH isoenzymes test, for more context.[1][3]
