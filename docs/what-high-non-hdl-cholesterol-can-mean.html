Non-HDL Cholesterol

What high non-HDL cholesterol can mean

Non-HDL cholesterol is a number on a lipid panel that captures all cholesterol outside of HDL, the "good" cholesterol. Because it includes LDL and other particles linked with plaque buildup, clinicians sometimes use it as a broader snapshot of cholesterol-related cardiovascular risk.[1][3][4]

Short answer: A high non-HDL result means the total amount of cholesterol carried on non-HDL particles is above the usual target. For many adults, general educational references list a healthy non-HDL level as below 130 mg/dL, but some people are given lower personal targets based on their risk history.[1][2][3][4]
Updated August 4, 2026

What non-HDL measures

MedlinePlus explains non-HDL in a simple way: it is your total cholesterol minus your HDL. That means it includes LDL and other cholesterol-carrying particles such as VLDL.[1]

MedlinePlus also notes that HDL, LDL, and VLDL are all lipoproteins. So non-HDL is not a separate kind of cholesterol by itself. It is a way of grouping the cholesterol carried on several non-HDL particles into one number.[2]

Cleveland Clinic describes non-HDL as the cholesterol carried on particles known to contribute to plaque buildup. In plain English, it is a wider measure than LDL alone.[4]

How high is high?

MedlinePlus lists a general healthy non-HDL level below 130 mg/dL for adults. That is the broad reference range many people see on educational materials and some lab reports.[1]

Mayo Clinic also says an optimal non-HDL level for most people is less than 130 mg/dL. But Mayo adds an important nuance: people with a history of heart attack may be given a lower target.[3]

  • For many adults: below 130 mg/dL is the usual general target.[1][3][4]
  • For people at higher cardiovascular risk: the goal may be lower.[3][4]
  • Non-HDL is often considered alongside LDL rather than instead of LDL.[1][4]

Why providers pay attention to it

LDL gets most of the attention, but non-HDL can be useful because it captures more than LDL alone. MedlinePlus says it includes LDL plus other particles such as VLDL, and Cleveland Clinic notes that these are the particles associated with plaque buildup.[1][4]

That broader view can matter when triglycerides are part of the story. The CDC says high triglycerides together with low HDL and/or high LDL can increase the risk for health problems such as heart attack.[5]

What can push non-HDL higher

Non-HDL rises when more cholesterol is being carried on "bad" lipoproteins. MedlinePlus lists several common factors that affect cholesterol levels, including diet, body weight, physical inactivity, smoking, age, and family history.[1]

Cleveland Clinic adds that genetics can play a major role and notes that foods high in saturated fat, trans fat, and added sugar can raise non-HDL-related lipoproteins.[4]

None of that means one high result reveals the exact cause. It means follow-up often focuses on the pattern, not just the number.[1][4][5]

What else may come up at follow-up

A high non-HDL result usually leads back to the full lipid panel. Providers may look at LDL, HDL, triglycerides, family history, and whether your personal goal should be lower than the general reference target.[1][3][4][5]

  • How your LDL, HDL, and triglycerides fit together.[1][5]
  • Whether your personal target is lower than 130 mg/dL.[3][4]
  • Whether family history or earlier heart disease changes interpretation.[1][3]
  • Whether repeat testing is needed for a clearer baseline.[1][5]

Questions you may want to ask at follow-up

  • How does my non-HDL number compare with my LDL and triglycerides?
  • Is my target the standard one, or should mine be lower?
  • Does my family history change how you read this result?
  • Do I need repeat testing or a fasting recheck?

FAQ

Is non-HDL cholesterol the same thing as LDL?

No. MedlinePlus says non-HDL is your total cholesterol minus HDL, so it includes LDL plus other particles such as VLDL.[1]

Does one high non-HDL result diagnose heart disease?

No. A high non-HDL result is a risk marker, not a diagnosis by itself. Providers usually interpret it with the rest of the lipid panel and your broader health picture.[1][3][4][5]

Why might my target be lower than 130 mg/dL?

Mayo Clinic and Cleveland Clinic both note that people with prior heart attack or higher cardiovascular risk may be given lower non-HDL goals than the broad reference used for many adults.[3][4]
