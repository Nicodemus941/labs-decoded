Total Protein

What high total protein can mean

A total protein blood test measures the combined amount of albumin and globulins in the fluid part of your blood. One common reference range is about 6.5 to 8.1 g/dL, but ranges can vary a little by lab.[1]

Short answer: High total protein is a lab finding, not a diagnosis by itself. It can show up with dehydration, inflammation, infection, immune-protein changes, or some blood disorders, so providers usually interpret it with albumin, globulins, and follow-up testing for context.[1][2][3][4]

Updated: August 29, 2026

What total protein measures

MedlinePlus says the total protein test measures two broad protein groups in blood: albumin and globulin.[1] Cleveland Clinic also describes high blood protein as high protein levels in blood plasma rather than one single substance rising on its own.[3]

That matters because a high total protein result does not tell you which protein fraction is driving the number. It is usually the start of a conversation, not the end of one.[1][2][3]

What albumin and globulins do

MedlinePlus explains that albumin helps keep fluid from leaking out of blood vessels and also carries substances through the bloodstream.[1] Cleveland Clinic adds that albumin is made by the liver.[3]

Globulins are a different protein group. MedlinePlus says they are an important part of the immune system, and Cleveland Clinic notes that globulins come from the liver and the immune system.[1][3]

Why a high result may appear on routine bloodwork

Mayo Clinic says high blood protein is not a specific disease or condition, and it is often found while checking another condition or symptom.[2] Cleveland Clinic says many people learn about it after a comprehensive metabolic panel or another routine blood test.[3]

In other words, this kind of result often needs more context before it becomes meaningful. Providers may want to know whether the change is coming from albumin, globulins, or both, and whether other labs point in the same direction.[1][2][3]

Broad reason-patterns clinicians may think about

A high total protein result can happen for several broad reasons. The safest way to think about it is as a clue that may need explanation, not as a one-number answer.[1][2][3]

  1. Concentration changes. Dehydration can make the fluid part of blood more concentrated, which can make total protein appear higher.[2][4]
  2. Inflammation or infection. MedlinePlus and Cleveland Clinic both list chronic inflammation and infections as possible reasons protein levels can run high.[1][3]
  3. Immune-protein shifts. Mayo Clinic notes that certain proteins can rise while the body is fighting infection or inflammation, and Cleveland Clinic points to higher immunoglobulin levels as one reason providers may order follow-up testing.[2][3]
  4. Some blood disorders. The approved source lane also notes that some blood disorders can be part of the picture, which is one reason a single high result should not be read in isolation.[1][2][3]

Why dehydration can affect the number

Dehydration is worth calling out because it is common and easy to misunderstand. Mayo Clinic says high blood protein can show up in people who are dehydrated because the blood plasma is more concentrated.[2]

The University of Rochester Medical Center makes the same point in simpler lab language: dehydration can make protein levels appear higher than they really are.[4] But that does not mean dehydration is always the answer. It means concentration is one possibility that has to be read alongside the rest of the labs and the clinical picture.[2][4]

Why repeat labs or follow-up tests may matter

Because high total protein is not a diagnosis by itself, providers may order more testing to find out what is behind it. Mayo Clinic mentions follow-up testing such as serum protein electrophoresis.[2]

Cleveland Clinic says additional testing can include protein electrophoresis or total immunoglobulin levels to help sort out whether the result reflects dehydration, inflammation, immune proteins, or another underlying issue.[3]

Questions worth asking at follow-up

If your total protein result was high, these are the kinds of questions that can help keep the follow-up grounded:

  • Was the number high because albumin was high, globulins were high, or both?
  • Did the rest of the bloodwork suggest dehydration, inflammation, infection, or something else?
  • Does my provider want repeat labs, serum protein electrophoresis, or total immunoglobulin testing for more context?

FAQ

Does high total protein mean I eat too much protein?

No. Mayo Clinic says a high-protein diet does not cause high blood protein. Providers usually interpret the result in the context of hydration, albumin and globulins, the rest of the bloodwork, and possible follow-up testing.[1][2]

Can dehydration make total protein look high?

Yes. Dehydration can make total protein look higher because the plasma in your blood becomes more concentrated. But dehydration is only one possible explanation, so the number still needs context.[2][4]

Why would my provider order more testing?

Because total protein is a signal, not a diagnosis by itself. Follow-up testing can help show whether albumin, globulins, inflammation, immune proteins, or other context explains why the result was high.[2][3]
