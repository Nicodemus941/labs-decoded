What low A/G ratio can mean

The albumin/globulin ratio, often shortened to A/G ratio, compares albumin with globulins, two major protein groups in your blood.[1]

Short answer: A low A/G ratio means the balance between albumin and globulins has shifted. That can happen when albumin is lower than expected compared with globulins, when globulins are higher relative to albumin, or both. MedlinePlus says a low A/G ratio can be seen with autoimmune disease, liver disease, and kidney disease, while albumin and globulin testing can add other clues about infection, nutrition, digestive conditions, and immune-protein changes.[1][2][3][4]

Updated: August 30, 2026

What the A/G ratio measures

MedlinePlus says a total protein and A/G ratio test measures total protein in your blood and also compares the amount of albumin with the amount of globulin.[1]

That means the ratio is about balance, not just one protein in isolation. A low ratio does not diagnose a single condition by itself. It tells providers that the relationship between these protein groups deserves a closer look.[1][3]

What albumin and globulins do

Albumin is a protein made by the liver. MedlinePlus says it helps keep fluid from leaking out of blood vessels and helps carry substances like hormones, vitamins, and enzymes through the body.[1][2]

Globulins are a group of proteins in the blood. MedlinePlus says some are made by the liver and others by the immune system, and they play roles in liver and kidney function, blood clotting, and fighting infection.[1][3] Cleveland Clinic also notes that globulins are tied to immune-system activity.[4]

Why a low A/G ratio may happen

The ratio can fall for more than one reason, which is why it helps to think in broad patterns instead of jumping to one explanation.[1][2][3]

  1. Albumin may be lower. MedlinePlus says low albumin can be seen with liver disease, kidney disease, malnutrition, infection, and digestive diseases that affect how your body uses protein from food.[1][2]
  2. Globulins may be relatively higher. MedlinePlus and Cleveland Clinic both describe globulin testing as a way to look for immune-system activity, infection, autoimmune disease, and other conditions that can change globulin levels.[3][4]
  3. Both sides of the ratio may be shifting. A provider may need the rest of the protein panel to tell whether the main issue is low albumin, higher globulins, or a mixed pattern.[1][3][4]

Broad reason-patterns clinicians may think about

MedlinePlus specifically says a low A/G ratio may be caused by autoimmune disease, liver disease, or kidney disease.[1] Albumin and globulin testing broadens that picture a little more.[2][3][4]

  • Liver context. Albumin is made by the liver, so liver disease can be one reason the ratio trends low.[1][2]
  • Kidney context. Kidney disease can lower albumin, and providers may compare the ratio with other kidney clues.[1][2][3]
  • Autoimmune or infection context. Higher globulin activity can show up with autoimmune disease or infection.[1][3][4]
  • Nutrition or digestive context. Low albumin can also happen with malnutrition or digestive diseases that affect absorption.[1][2]

None of those patterns are a diagnosis by themselves. They are reasons a provider may read the ratio together with symptoms, history, and the rest of the lab panel.[1][2][4]

Why the rest of the protein picture matters

A low A/G ratio makes more sense when you also know the total protein, albumin, and globulin values around it. MedlinePlus describes the total protein and A/G ratio test as part of a broader workup for liver, kidney, and nutritional problems.[1]

Albumin and globulin tests are also commonly part of panels such as a comprehensive metabolic panel or liver-related bloodwork.[2][3][4] That broader context helps show whether the ratio is part of a larger protein pattern or a smaller shift that still needs clarification.

Why follow-up testing may happen

MedlinePlus says providers may order separate albumin or globulin testing when total protein or the A/G ratio is not normal.[1]

The same MedlinePlus page says globulin follow-up can include serum electrophoresis, which measures different types of globulins.[1] Cleveland Clinic also describes serum protein electrophoresis as a way to get more detail about immune-related proteins when more context is needed.[4]

Questions worth asking at follow-up

If you saw a low A/G ratio on your report, these are the kinds of grounding questions that can help:

  • Was the ratio low mainly because albumin was low, globulins were higher, or both?
  • Did the rest of the bloodwork point more toward liver, kidney, immune, or nutrition context?
  • Does my provider want repeat labs, separate albumin and globulin testing, or serum protein electrophoresis for more detail?

FAQ

Does a low A/G ratio mean liver disease?

No. MedlinePlus says a low A/G ratio can happen with liver disease, but it can also be caused by kidney disease or autoimmune disease.[1] Albumin and globulin testing can add other context, including infection, nutrition, or digestive issues.[2][3][4]

Can a low A/G ratio happen because albumin is low?

Yes. MedlinePlus says low albumin can be seen with liver disease, kidney disease, malnutrition, infection, and digestive diseases that affect absorption, so a lower albumin level can pull the ratio down.[1][2]

Why might my provider order more protein testing?

Because the ratio is a clue, not a diagnosis. More testing can help show whether albumin, globulins, or specific globulin fractions are driving the result.[1][3][4]
