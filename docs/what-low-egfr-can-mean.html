Kidney test guide

What low eGFR can mean

Estimated glomerular filtration rate, or eGFR, is a number that helps show how well the kidneys filter blood. A low result can be a sign of reduced kidney function, but one result by itself does not confirm chronic kidney disease. Providers interpret it alongside other tests and results over time.

Updated September 30, 2026
Quick answer: NIDDK says an eGFR below 60 may mean kidney disease. Chronic kidney disease is generally diagnosed when evidence of kidney damage or reduced kidney function lasts more than three months. Urine albumin testing and earlier eGFR results can add important context.

What does eGFR measure?

GFR stands for glomerular filtration rate. It describes how much blood the kidneys filter over time. Because measuring GFR directly is complicated, labs usually report an estimate calculated from a blood test—most often creatinine, and sometimes cystatin C. Results are commonly shown in mL/min/1.73 m².

The word estimated matters: eGFR is not a direct measurement and is not a complete picture of kidney health on its own. The number is most useful when read with the reason the test was ordered, other kidney tests, and the pattern across more than one result.

What can a low eGFR mean?

A low eGFR can mean the kidneys are filtering less than expected. An eGFR below 60 may be a sign of kidney disease, but a single reading does not show whether a change is temporary or ongoing. NIDDK describes chronic kidney disease as generally involving evidence of kidney damage or reduced kidney function that persists for more than three months.

Kidney damage can also show up as albumin—a protein—in the urine. That is why a provider may look at eGFR together with a urine albumin test, often reported as a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR). An eGFR number should not be treated as a diagnosis or interpreted in isolation.

Why might one eGFR result be different?

Many eGFR calculations use creatinine, a waste product related to normal muscle use. Creatinine levels can vary with muscle mass, food, age, and activity. MedlinePlus also notes that dehydration, intense exercise, and a diet high in meat can affect blood creatinine. Since creatinine may be used to calculate eGFR, a provider may consider these details when reviewing a result.

The estimate itself has limits, too. NIDDK notes that eGFR is not a precise measurement and that changes over time can be more informative than one result. Your lab's reference information, previous results, and other health details all matter.

What may a provider check next?

Follow-up depends on your situation. A provider may compare the result with earlier tests, repeat a blood test, check urine for albumin, or review other kidney-related labs. If an estimate based on creatinine does not fit the rest of the picture, they may consider whether another estimate, such as one using cystatin C, would help.

Questions you may want to bring to a follow-up include:

  • How does this result compare with my earlier eGFR results?
  • Was a urine albumin or UACR test done, and what did it show?
  • Could anything about the timing of the test affect my creatinine result?
  • Do I need repeat testing, and when should I follow up?

FAQ

Does one low eGFR result mean chronic kidney disease?

No. A single eGFR result cannot establish chronic kidney disease. NIDDK says it is generally diagnosed when evidence of kidney damage or reduced kidney function is present for more than three months.

What other tests may be used with eGFR?

Providers may use a urine albumin test, often reported as a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, along with blood tests and earlier results to assess kidney health.

Can creatinine affect eGFR?

Yes. Many eGFR estimates use blood creatinine. Muscle mass, diet, and activity can affect creatinine, so a provider may consider those details and other tests when interpreting an estimate.
