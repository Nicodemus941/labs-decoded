Ferritin

What low ferritin can mean

Ferritin is a protein that stores iron. When ferritin is low, it usually means your body's iron reserves are low, even if the rest of the iron panel has not fully changed yet.[1][2][3]

Short answer: Low ferritin usually points to low iron stores. That can happen with blood loss, low iron intake, pregnancy, or trouble absorbing iron. Clinicians often compare ferritin with CBC and other iron tests to see whether low iron has already progressed to anemia and to look for the reason the stores are low.[2][4][5][6][7]

What ferritin measures

MedlinePlus says a ferritin blood test measures the amount of ferritin in your blood. Because ferritin stores iron, the test helps estimate how much iron your body has in reserve.[1]

Mayo Clinic and Cleveland Clinic make the same practical point in plainer terms: if ferritin is low, your iron stores are low.[2][3]

Why a low ferritin result matters

Low ferritin often shows up before full iron deficiency anemia does. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements says ferritin can identify low iron status during the first stage of iron depletion, before anemia starts.[4]

That is why a low ferritin result can still matter even when hemoglobin is not yet clearly low. It is a clue that the body may be running short on stored iron.[4][7]

Common reasons ferritin runs low

  • Heavy menstrual bleeding or other blood loss.[1][2][5]
  • Bleeding somewhere in the digestive tract.[5][7]
  • A diet that is too low in iron.[5]
  • Trouble absorbing iron from food, vitamins, or medicines.[5]
  • Pregnancy or other situations where iron needs are higher.[2]

MedlinePlus also notes that ferritin testing may be used in people at higher risk for iron deficiency, including people with heavy menstrual bleeding or who are underweight.[1]

What symptoms can go with low ferritin or low iron

Symptoms are not specific, but MedlinePlus and Cleveland Clinic both list fatigue, weakness, dizziness, shortness of breath, pale skin, and a fast heartbeat among common signs of iron levels that are too low.[1][2][6]

Symptoms alone cannot tell you whether ferritin is the whole explanation. They are one reason clinicians usually look at the full history and the rest of the lab work, not just one number.[6][7]

Why providers often order more than one iron-related test

Ferritin is important, but it is not the only iron test. MedlinePlus says iron testing can also include serum iron, transferrin, and total iron-binding capacity. Providers use these tests together to understand whether iron is low, whether anemia is present, and whether treatment appears to be working.[6]

Cleveland Clinic also notes that ferritin is often checked when a CBC shows low hemoglobin or hematocrit, or when symptoms and risk factors make iron deficiency more likely.[2]

Reference ranges can vary

MedlinePlus Medical Encyclopedia lists common ferritin reference ranges of 30 to 400 ng/mL for males and 13 to 150 ng/mL for females, but it also says ranges vary between laboratories.[5]

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements adds that a serum ferritin level below 30 mcg/L suggests iron deficiency, while a value below 10 mcg/L suggests iron deficiency anemia.[4]

Questions worth asking at follow-up

  • Is ferritin low on its own, or are hemoglobin, hematocrit, or other iron tests low too?
  • Could blood loss, heavy periods, pregnancy, diet, or absorption issues be part of the picture?
  • Do I need repeat ferritin testing or a fuller iron panel after follow-up?
  • Should the possible source of low iron be investigated further?

FAQ

Does low ferritin always mean anemia?

No. Low ferritin often means iron stores are low, but the NIH says ferritin can fall before iron deficiency anemia develops. That is why clinicians often compare ferritin with CBC results and other iron tests.[4][6][7]

Can ferritin be low even if hemoglobin is normal?

Yes. Ferritin can identify early iron depletion before anemia appears.[4]

Why might my provider order more tests after a low ferritin result?

Because low ferritin is a clue, not a complete diagnosis. Providers may want a CBC, other iron tests, or evaluation for blood loss or poor absorption.[2][6][7]
