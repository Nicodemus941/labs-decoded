Sodium

What low sodium can mean

A sodium blood test measures the amount of sodium in your blood. Sodium is an electrolyte that helps control fluid balance and supports nerve and muscle function. Your body usually keeps sodium in a narrow range, and one common reference range is about 134 to 144 mmol/L.[1][5]

Short answer: A low sodium result can happen for several different reasons, including losing sodium through vomiting or diarrhea, holding onto extra body water, taking certain medicines, or having an underlying condition that changes fluid balance. A low number alone does not tell you which cause is responsible.[1][2][3][4]

What the test is really measuring

Sodium is not just about table salt. In blood testing, sodium is mainly a fluid-balance marker. Providers often think of low sodium, also called hyponatremia, as a clue that the balance between water and sodium has shifted, not simply that someone ate too little salt.[1][2][4]

MedlinePlus notes that sodium is often checked as part of an electrolyte panel, BMP, or CMP. That matters because a low sodium result is usually interpreted together with other electrolytes and the rest of the clinical picture.[1]

Common patterns behind low sodium

  1. Too much water relative to sodium. This is one of the biggest reasons a sodium level falls. Official sources describe low sodium as a problem that often happens when the body holds onto extra fluid, which dilutes sodium in the bloodstream.[1][2][4]
  2. Fluid loss from vomiting or diarrhea. Sodium can also fall when the body loses both fluid and sodium, especially with gastrointestinal losses.[1][2][4]
  3. Medicine effects. Mayo Clinic and Cleveland Clinic both list certain medicines as common contributors, especially diuretics, some antidepressants, and some pain medicines.[1][3][4]
  4. Heart, kidney, liver, hormone, or other medical conditions. Official sources list heart failure, kidney disease, liver disease, SIADH, hypothyroidism, Addison disease, and other conditions as possibilities depending on the full pattern.[1][2][3][4]

Why symptoms matter so much

A sodium number is not interpreted in a vacuum. Official sources list weakness, fatigue, confusion, muscle cramps or twitching, nausea, vomiting, headaches, and seizures among the symptoms that can show up with hyponatremia.[1][3][4]

Severe low sodium can be dangerous. Cleveland Clinic notes that untreated severe hyponatremia can lead to brain swelling, coma, or death, and its emergency-warning list includes confusion, seizures, difficulty waking up, severe headache, and severe vomiting or diarrhea.[4]

That does not mean every low sodium result is an emergency. It means the level, the symptoms, and how quickly the sodium changed all matter when a provider decides how urgent the situation is.[3][4]

Why one low sodium result does not diagnose the cause

MedlinePlus says providers usually interpret sodium together with other electrolytes and related lab results.[1] Cleveland Clinic also notes that blood tests and urine tests are often used to help figure out what type of hyponatremia is present and what may be driving it.[4]

In practical terms, providers often ask questions like:

  • Are there symptoms such as confusion, vomiting, or severe fatigue?
  • Did the number drop suddenly or has it likely been present longer?
  • Is there a recent history of diarrhea, vomiting, or heavy fluid intake?
  • Could a medicine be contributing?
  • Do other lab results point toward kidney, liver, heart, or hormone issues?

That is why a low sodium result is usually the start of interpretation, not the end of it.[1][3][4]

FAQ

Does low sodium always mean you are not eating enough salt?

No. Low sodium is often more about water balance than salt intake alone. It can happen when the body holds onto extra water, loses sodium through vomiting or diarrhea, or reacts to medicines or other medical conditions.[1][2][4]

Can medicines cause a low sodium result?

Yes. Official sources specifically list diuretics and also mention some antidepressants and pain medicines as drugs that can contribute to hyponatremia.[1][3][4]

Why do providers order other tests with a low sodium result?

Because the sodium number does not reveal one exact cause by itself. Providers often compare it with other electrolytes and may use urine or additional blood tests to see whether the pattern fits fluid overload, sodium loss, medicine effect, or another underlying condition.[1][4]
