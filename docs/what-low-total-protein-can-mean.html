Total Protein

What low total protein can mean

A total protein test measures the total amount of protein in your blood. That result includes albumin and globulins, so a low number is a broad signal that may need more context rather than a one-step answer.[1][2]

Short answer: Low total protein can happen for different reasons, including liver, kidney, nutrition, absorption, inflammation, and pregnancy-related context. Providers often look at albumin, globulins, other bloodwork, and the bigger clinical picture before deciding what the result may mean.[1][2][3][4]
Updated August 28, 2026

What total protein measures

MedlinePlus says a total protein and albumin/globulin ratio test measures the total amount of protein in your blood.[1] It is often included in a comprehensive metabolic panel, also called a CMP, so it may show up in routine bloodwork even when protein itself was not the main reason for testing.[1]

The key point is that total protein is a combined result. It does not tell the whole story by itself, because the two major blood-protein groups do different jobs and may not move in the same direction.[1][2]

What albumin does

Albumin makes up most of the protein in the blood.[1] MedlinePlus says it helps keep fluid from leaking out of blood vessels and also helps move hormones, medicines, vitamins, and other important substances through the body.[1]

Cleveland Clinic describes albumin as one of the two main blood proteins and also emphasizes its role in keeping fluid from leaking out of the bloodstream.[3] That is one reason providers often pay close attention to albumin when total protein is low, but low total protein is not automatically the same thing as low albumin alone.[1][3]

What globulins do

Globulins are the other major blood-protein group.[1] MedlinePlus says globulins help fight infection and move nutrients throughout the body.[1][2]

The MedlinePlus globulin test page also notes that some globulins are made by the liver while others are made by the immune system.[2] That is why a low total protein result may lead to a closer look at globulins instead of assuming albumin is the whole explanation.[1][2]

Where low total protein may show up in routine bloodwork

Because total protein is often part of a CMP, a low result may first show up on routine labs.[1] It can also come up when a provider is checking problems that affect protein balance, fluid balance, the liver, the kidneys, or overall nutrition.[1][4]

MedlinePlus lists examples of symptoms that can go along with abnormal protein levels, including swelling, fatigue, unexplained weight loss, appetite loss, nausea, vomiting, jaundice, or blood in the urine.[1] Those symptoms are not specific to one cause, but they help explain why the result is usually interpreted with the rest of the story instead of on its own.[1][3]

Broad reason-patterns clinicians may think about

Official sources keep the causes broad for a reason: low total protein can happen in more than one setting.[1][3][4] The useful frame is not “this proves one disease,” but “this result may fit with several kinds of clinical context.”[1][3]

  • Liver-related context, because the liver helps make important blood proteins.[1][2][3]
  • Kidney-related context, especially when protein balance is affected or protein is lost.[1][2][3][4]
  • Nutrition or absorption-related context, including malnutrition or malabsorption syndromes.[1][2][3][4]
  • Inflammation, infection, autoimmune disease, or other chronic illness that changes protein needs or protein balance.[2][3]
  • Pregnancy or breastfeeding context, where protein needs can be different.[3][4]

Labcorp also notes that low total protein can be seen with pregnancy, intravenous fluids, liver disease, some kidney disorders, protein-losing enteropathies, Crohn's disease, chronic ulcerative colitis, starvation, malabsorption, malnutrition, burns, severe skin disease, and other chronic diseases.[4] That long list is another reminder that one low number does not identify one cause by itself.[4]

Why other tests or repeat labs may matter

Follow-up testing helps sort out where the low result may be coming from.[1][2][3][4] A provider may look more closely at albumin, globulins, an albumin/globulin ratio, or other CMP results to see whether the change seems more connected to liver, kidney, nutrition, or another part of the picture.[1][2]

  • Albumin and globulin details, because total protein covers both rather than just one of them.[1][2]
  • Other CMP results that may add liver, kidney, or nutrition context.[1][2][3]
  • Repeat testing when the first result needs confirmation or more trend context.[1][3][4]
  • More specific protein testing when the fractions of total protein need a closer look.[2][4]

Labcorp specifically notes that interpretation is improved when the fractions that make up total protein are examined when clinically indicated.[4] In plain language, that means the combined number is often only the starting point.[4]

Questions worth asking at follow-up

  • Was the low total protein result mainly about albumin, globulins, or both?
  • Did any other CMP, liver, kidney, or urine results move in the same direction?
  • Does my history suggest nutrition, absorption, inflammation, liver, kidney, or pregnancy-related context that could help explain the result?
  • Would repeat labs or more specific protein testing add useful context before drawing conclusions?

FAQ

Does low total protein always mean poor nutrition?

No. Poor nutrition can be one reason for a low total protein result, but it is not the only possible explanation. Providers usually read total protein alongside albumin, globulins, other bloodwork, and the broader health context before deciding what it may mean.[1][3]

Is low total protein the same as low albumin?

Not exactly. Total protein includes both albumin and globulins, so a low total protein result is not automatically the same thing as low albumin alone. Looking at albumin, globulins, or an A/G ratio can help show where the change may be coming from.[1][2]

Why would my provider order more testing?

A provider may want more testing because total protein is only one part of the picture. Follow-up labs can help show whether albumin, globulins, other CMP results, or repeat testing add context before drawing conclusions from one number.[1][2][3][4]

Bottom line

A low total protein result can be meaningful, but it does not explain itself. The most useful read usually comes from seeing how albumin, globulins, symptoms, and other labs fit together instead of treating one number as a final answer.[1][2][3][4]
