Vitamin B12

What low vitamin B12 can mean

Vitamin B12 helps keep blood cells and nerve cells healthy. A low result can happen when you are not getting enough B12, but it can also happen when your body has trouble absorbing it.[1][2][3]

Short answer: A low vitamin B12 result may point to a true deficiency, especially if symptoms or follow-up tests support it. Clinicians often look at B12 together with symptoms, blood counts, and sometimes methylmalonic acid, also called MMA.[2][4]
Updated July 18, 2026

What vitamin B12 does

The National Institutes of Health says vitamin B12 helps keep your body's blood and nerve cells healthy and also helps make DNA.[1] When B12 runs low, the body can have trouble making healthy red blood cells, and nerve symptoms can show up too.[1][3]

Mayo Clinic notes that not having enough vitamin B12 can contribute to vitamin deficiency anemia, a condition where red blood cells become too large and do not work as well as they should.[3]

What counts as low

MedlinePlus lists a typical normal vitamin B12 range as 299 to 1054 pg/mL, while also noting that ranges can vary a bit by laboratory.[2] On that same reference page, values below 299 pg/mL are described as a possible sign of vitamin B12 deficiency.[2]

A low or borderline result is not always the end of the story. MedlinePlus says clinicians may confirm a suspected deficiency with an MMA test, because high MMA can be a sign of true vitamin B12 deficiency.[2][4]

Common reasons a B12 result may be low

A low B12 result can happen for more than one reason. MedlinePlus and Mayo Clinic both point to low intake in strict vegetarian or vegan diets, but they also emphasize absorption problems as a major part of the picture.[2][3][5]

  • Not getting enough B12 from food, which is more likely with very low animal-food intake.[1][2]
  • Digestive conditions such as celiac disease or Crohn's disease that make absorption harder.[2][3][4][5]
  • Pernicious anemia or other problems with intrinsic factor, which the body needs to absorb B12 well.[2][3][5]
  • Past stomach or intestinal surgery that changes how B12 is absorbed.[3][5]
  • Older age, which can make absorption harder for some people.[1][4][5]

Symptoms clinicians may ask about

Low B12 can affect both blood and nerves. Across NIH, MedlinePlus, and Mayo Clinic sources, symptoms linked to deficiency can include tiredness, weakness, numbness or tingling in the hands and feet, balance problems, dizziness, pale skin, and changes in thinking or mood.[1][3][4][5]

Symptoms are one reason clinicians usually do not interpret a B12 result in isolation. The number matters, but the rest of the history matters too.[2][3]

Why follow-up testing may happen

If B12 is low or borderline, follow-up testing can help sort out whether the result reflects a true deficiency and what might be causing it. MedlinePlus specifically notes MMA as a confirmation step.[2][4]

  • Vitamin B12 level, especially if the first result needs context.[2]
  • MMA, which can rise when B12 is truly low.[2][4]
  • Blood count findings, since B12 deficiency can be linked to anemia.[1][3]
  • History about diet, digestive disease, medications, or prior surgery.[1][3][5]

Food sources of vitamin B12

NIH and MedlinePlus say vitamin B12 is naturally found in animal foods such as fish, meat, poultry, eggs, milk, and other dairy products.[1][5] Some breakfast cereals and nutritional yeast products are fortified with B12.[1][5]

Because plant foods do not naturally contain vitamin B12 unless they are fortified, people who avoid animal foods may need extra planning around B12 intake.[1][5]

Bottom line

A low vitamin B12 result can be meaningful, but it does not explain itself. The most useful next step is usually to interpret the result alongside symptoms, diet, medical history, and any follow-up testing your clinician recommends.[2][3][4]
