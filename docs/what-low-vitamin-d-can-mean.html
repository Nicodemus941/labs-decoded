Vitamin D

What low vitamin D can mean

A vitamin D blood test usually measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D, written as 25(OH)D. When that level is low, it often means your body is not getting, absorbing, or processing enough vitamin D the way clinicians would expect.[1]

Short answer: Low vitamin D can happen with limited sunlight, low dietary intake, trouble absorbing nutrients, or liver or kidney problems that affect how vitamin D is processed. The result is usually interpreted alongside symptoms, risk factors, and sometimes other labs.[1][2][3][4]
Updated July 20, 2026

What the test measures

MedlinePlus says the routine blood test checks 25(OH)D because it is the most accurate way to tell whether you have enough vitamin D. A different test for active vitamin D exists, but it is not usually the one used to check everyday vitamin D status.[1]

Vitamin D helps your body absorb calcium and supports healthy bones and teeth. MedlinePlus and the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements also note that vitamin D helps muscles, nerves, and the immune system work normally.[1][2]

What can push vitamin D lower

  • Limited sunlight exposure.[1][2][3]
  • Too little vitamin D from food or supplements.[1][2][3]
  • Conditions that make it harder to absorb nutrients, such as some bowel disorders.[1][3]
  • Liver or kidney disease that affects vitamin D processing.[1]
  • Medicines that can affect vitamin D levels.[1]

MedlinePlus also notes that some people are tested because they have a higher chance of deficiency, including people with low sun exposure, malnutrition, weight-loss surgery, or conditions that reduce absorption.[1]

Why low vitamin D matters

Very low vitamin D can weaken bones. MedlinePlus says deficiency can lead to bone disorders, and Mayo Clinic notes that not getting enough vitamin D can make bones thin, brittle, bent, or bowed over time.[1][3]

Cleveland Clinic explains that chronic or severe deficiency can reduce calcium and phosphorus absorption, which may contribute to muscle weakness, cramps, osteomalacia in adults, and rickets in children.[4]

Why symptoms can be vague

Low vitamin D does not always cause clear symptoms. When symptoms do happen, MedlinePlus lists bone pain, muscle weakness, aches, weak bones, or fractures among the reasons a provider might order testing.[1]

That is one reason the number is not interpreted in isolation. A mildly low result in someone without symptoms may lead to a different conversation than a low result in someone with bone symptoms, absorption problems, or kidney or liver disease.[1][3]

How reports often describe the result

MedlinePlus says vitamin D reports often group results into categories such as deficient, insufficient, sufficient, or toxic. The wording can vary by lab, so the reference range printed on your own report matters.[1]

If a result is low, MedlinePlus says it may reflect low intake, low sunlight exposure, absorption problems, liver or kidney disease, or a medicine effect. That is why providers usually ask about the full context before drawing conclusions.[1]

Questions you may want to ask at follow-up

  • Was this routine screening, or was it ordered because of symptoms or another condition?
  • Could diet, sunlight exposure, bowel issues, or medicines be affecting the result?
  • Do any other labs or health conditions change how this number should be interpreted?
  • Should this level be rechecked after follow-up?

FAQ

Does a low vitamin D result always cause symptoms?

No. Some people are tested because they have bone pain or muscle weakness, while others have few or no obvious symptoms. The importance of the result depends on the level and the rest of the clinical picture.[1][4]

What kind of vitamin D blood test is usually used?

Usually a 25-hydroxyvitamin D test. MedlinePlus says this form, 25(OH)D, is the most accurate routine measure of vitamin D status.[1]

Why might my provider ask about supplements before interpreting the test?

MedlinePlus says medicines, vitamins, and supplements can affect results. Too much vitamin D from supplements can also be harmful, so context matters on both the low and high side.[1][3]
